If you’ve been diagnosed with PCOS, or wondered whether you have it, you may start hearing a different name: PMOS, short for polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome. PMOS (formerly PCOS) is the same condition. The name has changed to describe it more accurately.

## Why the old name didn’t fit

Polycystic ovary syndrome was named after what doctors saw on ultrasound: many small follicles on the ovaries. But those follicles aren’t really cysts, not everyone with the condition has them, and the name left out the parts that affect people most day to day.

## What the new name says

- **Polyendocrine:** it involves several hormones, not only ovarian ones. Insulin and androgens (hormones like testosterone) are central.
- **Metabolic:** it affects how the body handles blood sugar and energy. Insulin resistance is common, including in people who aren’t overweight.
- **Ovarian:** it still affects ovulation and cycles.

The change followed years of consultation with patients and clinicians around the world, many of whom felt the old name led to missed diagnoses and care that focused only on periods or fertility.

## Why it matters for you

Seeing it as a whole-body condition changes the conversation. It means asking about blood sugar, heart health, mood and sleep, not just cycles. It also explains why symptoms that seem unrelated, like irregular periods, acne, cravings and fatigue, often travel together.

## If you think it might be you

It’s common, often estimated to affect around 1 in 10 women of reproductive age, and many are never diagnosed. Irregular or missing periods, acne or extra hair growth, and difficulty getting pregnant are common reasons people are first checked. Your doctor can assess it with your history, an exam and blood tests.
