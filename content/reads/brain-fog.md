Brain fog isn’t a medical diagnosis, but most people know it when it happens: losing words mid-sentence, rereading the same email, walking into a room and forgetting why. It can be unsettling. It’s also very common, and it often has causes you can do something about.

## Common causes

- **Sleep.** Even a few short nights in a row affect focus and memory.
- **Stress and overload.** When your mind is holding too much, there’s less room for everything else.
- **Hormone changes.** Some people notice fog before their period, after having a baby, or in perimenopause, when shifting estrogen affects the brain.
- **Low iron.** Iron deficiency, with or without anemia, is common in people with heavy periods and can cause tiredness and poor concentration.
- **Thyroid changes.** An underactive thyroid can slow thinking.
- **Low mood or anxiety.** Depression often shows up as trouble concentrating, not only sadness.
- **Long gaps without food or water.**

## What can help

- Protecting sleep and keeping a steady wake time
- Regular meals and water through the day
- Writing things down, so your memory doesn’t have to hold everything
- Noticing when the fog is worse, like certain days of your cycle or after bad nights

## When to talk to your doctor

If brain fog lasts for weeks, is getting worse, or comes with heavy periods, exhaustion, low mood or other changes, mention it. Simple blood tests, like iron (ferritin) and thyroid, can rule out common causes. Get urgent help for sudden confusion, weakness on one side, or trouble speaking.
